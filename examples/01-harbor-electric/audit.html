What this says

Of the twenty-six hours we put a name to -- out of the sixty-three you said you work -- sixteen are going to work that never needed your judgment: writing the same six paragraphs onto a different number, laying out a schedule grid on a Sunday night, and the texting wrapped around "are you coming Tuesday" calls. At your own effective rate, that is roughly $4,227 a month of your time. Thirteen and a half of those hours software genuinely carries; the other two and a half move to Angela. The hours come back to the two things nobody else at Harbor can do: standing on a job deciding how it gets built, and pricing the work.

The one line About sixteen hours a week of your time goes to work you should not be holding -- roughly $4,227 a month of your own time, most of it spent on paperwork wrapped around the real job.

Where the work goes

Nothing here removes a person from Harbor Electric. Every line moves a task: down to whoever owns that outcome, with AI carrying the mechanical part where there is one -- and a short summary coming back to you for approval. You keep every decision. You stop doing the typing.

Two lines below say no AI, and they mean it. Phone calls and site walks are not software work, and this page will not tell you otherwise. Those still move to Angela -- what changes is whose job they are, not whether a person does them.

What moves down, and to whom

Each of these already needs a judgment -- just not yours. It moves to the person who owns that outcome, and they get an assist that makes it a bigger job than it was, not a smaller one.

Routine scheduling calls → Angela (no AI — this one is just work)

Right now nobody formally owns the schedule -- Angela does it when she is in the office, which is why it lands back on you the rest of the time. Naming her the owner is the whole change here. Somebody still picks up the phone and says "yes, Tuesday" -- no software makes that call, and this page is not going to pretend otherwise. What moves is whose job it is. You get a Friday summary of anything that shifted. Angela goes from catching phones to owning the customer's week -- that is a promotion, and it is worth saying to her that way.

Confirmation and running-late texts → carried by AI

This is the part of those calls that is software work, and it was an hour and a half of your week. Confirmations go out off the schedule; a running-late note goes out when the crew slips. You see the week's worth in the Friday summary and never type one again.

Chasing the supply house → Angela (no AI on the calls)

Same owner. The calls are Angela's -- she decides who to lean on and how hard, and there is no software substitute for a person the counter guy knows. What AI takes is the list underneath it: what has slipped past its promised date, and the customer note that has to go out because of it, drafted for your approval. Anything that threatens a scheduled job comes up to you the same day.

Payroll → the bookkeeper

She already owns the outcome; you were doing the assembly. AI pulls the hours together into the format she wants, she runs it, and you approve the total instead of building it.

What stays yours

These need your relationships, your authority, or twenty years of knowing how a job goes sideways. This is the list the sixteen hours are for -- more site time, more pricing, more of the calls that turn into work.

  • Pricing the quote -- the margin call is the business, and it is a judgment nobody else at Harbor has the exposure to make.
  • Site visits and walkthroughs -- Hendricks and the church both needed somebody standing there deciding how it gets built.
  • New-customer calls -- these are the calls that become jobs. They should get more of you, not less.
  • The safety meeting -- it lands differently coming from the owner, and everyone in the room knows it.

How these numbers were figured

Every hour above came from Dave's own answers -- nothing was estimated. Both kinds of hand-down count at half, because handing work over and reading the summary still takes real time. A month is counted as 4.33 weeks. The $61 rate is Dave's own figure: $185,000 of owner pay divided by the same 63 hours a week, across 48 weeks. The 26 hours reviewed are the ones Dave could put a name to; his week is 63, and the rest of it is worth a second pass someday.

Where to start

  1. This week: the quote document. Five hours, one change, and you will feel it Friday. Feed the last five quotes in as the pattern, then draft the next one with the price blank.
  2. Next Sunday: the schedule grid. Let it lay out the week from the job list; you move names and approve. Target thirty minutes instead of two and a half hours.
  3. Tell Angela she owns the schedule. The conversation is the work here; the callback draft is the easy part.
  4. Then invoicing and the certificates. Small hours, but they are the ones that ruin a Friday.

Run this audit again in a quarter against the same list -- the second one shows what actually moved, and what crept back.
